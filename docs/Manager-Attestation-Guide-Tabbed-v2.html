You got an email saying you have access to review. Here is exactly what to do. You can read the rest of the guide later -- this gets you through your first review right now.

You cannot break anything

2. Making Decisions: Approve, Revoke, Reassign

Three actions you can take for each access item, plus a visual decision guide and a checklist for elevated access.

2.1 Approve

Click the checkmark icon next to an access item.

This means: "Yes, this person should have this access for their current job."

What happens: Nothing changes. The access stays in place. Your approval is recorded in the audit trail.

Note
Some items show Acknowledge instead of Approve. These are access items that were automatically assigned based on the person's job title or department. You cannot remove them through this review -- if the assignment seems wrong, contact the IAM team.

2.2 Revoke

Click the X icon next to an access item.

This means: "No, remove this access."

What happens after you sign off:

  • Connected systems (like Active Directory): the access is removed automatically
  • Other systems (apps without a direct connection): a task is created for the system owner to remove it manually

You can set a future removal date for roles ("Remove this on June 15 when the project ends"). This is optional and only available for roles, not individual permissions.

Mistakes are fixable
Before you sign off, you can click Revisit Decision to change any approval to a revocation or vice versa. After sign-off, if you made an error, contact the IAM team -- they can help restore access through the standard request process, which typically takes [X hours/days].

2.3 Reassign

If you are not the right person to evaluate an item, send it to someone who is.

Use reassign when:

  • You see a system you have never heard of -- send it to the person who manages that system
  • The team member recently transferred to you and their old manager knows better
  • You are going on leave and cannot finish by the deadline
  1. Click Reassign
  2. Choose the new reviewer
  3. Write a reason (include your name so they can follow up)

Rules: You cannot reassign to the person whose access is being reviewed (the system prevents self-review). You cannot reassign after you have signed off.

Tip
Reassigning is not a sign of failure -- it is the responsible thing to do when you do not have enough context to make a good decision. It is always better to reassign than to guess.

2.4 Bulk Decisions

You can select multiple items with checkboxes and click Bulk Decisions to approve or revoke them at once. This is useful for routine, low-risk items that you are confident about.

Use carefully for elevated access
Bulk-approving items flagged as "privileged" can appear as a lack of review. For elevated access, take a moment to evaluate each item individually.

2.5 Adding Comments

You can add a short note to any decision. Comments are required for some types of revocations and recommended for elevated access. A single sentence is enough:

  • "Employee transferred to Marketing June 1 -- no longer needs this"
  • "Active DBA; still primary administrator for these servers"
  • "Temporary access for Q2 project; project completed May 28"

2.7 Checklist for Elevated Access

For items flagged as "privileged," run through this quick checklist:

  • Current role match: Does this access fit their current job (not a previous role)?
  • Active use: Have they used it recently, or has it been sitting idle?
  • Minimum needed: Is this the least amount of access that works, or is there a narrower option?
  • Time-bound? Should this expire on a specific date, or is it ongoing?
  • Conflict check: Does approving this create a conflict with other access they have?
  • Peer comparison: Do others in the same role have this, or is this person an outlier?
Tip
You do not need every checkbox for every item. For routine access, a quick role-match check is enough. The full checklist is for elevated or unfamiliar items.

  • An administrator will handle your undecided items (typically by keeping access in place)

4. Common Scenarios

Eight real-world situations you might encounter and exactly how to handle them.

New hire with too much access Mixed

Your new team member Alex shows up with 8 permissions including server administration access. Alex is a junior developer -- the onboarding template was too broad.

What to do:
  1. Click on Alex to see all access items
  2. Revoke the admin access that does not match the junior developer role
  3. Approve the development tools that Alex actually needs
  4. Comment: "New hire; right-sizing to junior developer role"
Team member transferred in with old access Mixed

Jamie transferred from Infrastructure to your team 3 months ago. A new developer tool triggers this campaign, but you notice Jamie still has 4 infrastructure permissions.

What to do:
  1. Approve the new developer tool (matches current role)
  2. Revoke the 4 infrastructure permissions (leftover from old role)
  3. Comment on each: "Role transfer from Infrastructure; no longer needed"
"I only use it during emergencies" Revoke

Chris, a senior engineer, has administrator access. "I only use it during outages -- maybe once a quarter."

What to do:
  1. Revoke the standing admin access
  2. Work with the IAM team to set up an emergency access process for Chris
  3. Tell Chris: "We're replacing your standing access with an on-demand process. You'll get it when you need it."
Access that is clearly appropriate Approve

Database admin Pat has DB Admin access on three production servers that Pat manages daily.

What to do:
  1. Confirm Pat is still the active DBA for these servers (yes)
  2. Approve all three with a comment: "Active DBA; primary admin per current assignment"

Not every review ends in a revocation. Approving is the right answer when the access matches the job.

Access you do not recognize Reassign

You see "SCCM_PatchAdmin_Tier2" in your review. You have never heard of this system.

What to do:
  1. Reassign to the person who manages that system
  2. Note: "Reassigning -- I don't manage this system. Contact me at [your email] with questions."

Reassigning is always better than guessing.

Your team only uses CRM, but you see a bunch of other stuff Mixed

You manage a customer service team. You know they use the CRM and phone system. Your campaign shows 5 items -- you recognize 2 of them, the other 3 are system names you have never seen.

What to do:
  1. Approve the 2 items you recognize (CRM access, phone system)
  2. For the 3 items you do not recognize: either Reassign to a technical lead, or ask the team member "Do you use [system name]?" -- a quick Teams message or email is enough
  3. If they say "no" or "I don't know what that is" -- Revoke with a comment

You do not need to know every system. Approve what you are confident about, and get help with the rest.

You are a new manager and inherited a team Reassign / Approve

You were recently promoted or transferred. You get your first campaign with items for 15 people across systems you barely know. You do not yet know what your team does day-to-day.

What to do:
  1. Do not panic -- this is a normal situation for new managers
  2. For access you understand: make your best decision
  3. For everything else: Reassign to the team lead or the person's previous manager
  4. Contact the IAM team ([IAM-TEAM-EMAIL]) and let them know you are new to the role. They can help you understand what your team's typical access profile looks like.
You accidentally revoked something your team member needs Recovery

You revoked a permission, signed off, and now your team member cannot access a system they need for their job today.

What to do:
  1. If you have not signed off yet: click Revisit Decision and change the revocation to an approval
  2. If you already signed off: contact the IAM team immediately at [IAM-TEAM-EMAIL]
  3. The team member can submit a new access request, which typically takes [X hours/days] to process
  4. For urgent needs, the IAM team may be able to expedite the request

Mistakes happen. The important thing is to address them quickly. This is not a career-ending event.

5. Why We Review Access Daily

Understanding why daily reviews exist helps you appreciate the value of what you are doing. This section is optional background reading.

Traditional access reviews happen quarterly or annually. A user gets elevated access on January 3; the next review is April 1 -- for 88 days, that access sits unexamined. If the account is compromised, the impact could affect the entire organization.

Daily delta reviews (we review only new or changed access each day) flip this model:

ApproachHow Much to ReviewReviewer FatigueTime Until Review
Quarterly fullHundreds of itemsHigh -- leads to rubber-stampingUp to 90 days
Daily delta1-5 items (typical)Low -- focused, quick decisionsLess than 24 hours
Key point
Daily reviews are smaller, not more work. You review fewer items more frequently, which leads to better decisions and stronger compliance evidence.

What Triggers a Campaign for You

You receive a campaign only when someone on your team gets new elevated access -- for example, being added to an Active Directory group that grants administrative permissions. The system detects the change, groups affected team members by manager, and creates your review.

If nothing changed for your team, you get no campaign. No changes = no action needed.

6. Why Managers Review (Not Application Owners)

Understanding why you are being asked to review, rather than the IT team or the system administrator.

Some organizations have the system administrator review who has access to their system. The problem: the administrator knows the system but not the people. They cannot answer the most important question:

"Does this person still need this access for their current role?"

System Admin Review

"Is this account set up correctly?"

Cannot tell whether the person still needs the access for their job.

Manager Review

"Should this person have this access?"

You know your team, their projects, and whether access matches current work.

Only you can answer:

  • What is this team member working on right now?
  • Did they recently change roles or projects?
  • Was this access a temporary need that has expired?
  • Could they do their job with a lower level of access?
How it works
The system automatically sends each manager a review containing only their direct reports. You never see someone else's team. If a team member has no manager assigned in the HR system, the review goes to an administrator instead.

7. Talking to Your Team about Access

How to have productive conversations with your team members about their access rights -- especially when you need to revoke something.

7.1 Four Questions to Ask

When you are reviewing access for a team member -- or when they ask for new access -- these four questions will guide your decision:

The Four Questions
  1. "What specifically do you need to accomplish?"
    Understand the task. Often the real need is narrower than the access requested.
  2. "Is there a way to do this with less access?"
    Read-only instead of read-write? Delegated role instead of full admin?
  3. "How long will you need this?"
    "For this project" or "until next month" means it should have an end date.
  4. "What happens if you lose this access tomorrow?"
    If the answer is "nothing," that is your signal to revoke.
When you cannot ask directly
If you are reviewing at 8pm and the team member is offline, use the information in the review interface: check the "last activity" date (has this access been used recently?) and the "peer comparison" data (do others in the same role have this?).

7.2 Setting the Tone

Frame access reviews as professional hygiene -- something routine and reasonable, not a punishment:

Instead of...Try...
"I'm revoking your access""Let's right-size your access to match your current role"
"Security says you can't have this""We're reducing the team's risk exposure -- here's what that means"
"You don't need admin access""What specific tasks need admin access? Let's find the right level"

7.3 What to Tell Your Team

"We've moved to daily access reviews for elevated permissions. When you get new access, I'll review it within 24 hours. This is actually better for everyone -- instead of a big quarterly review, I'm looking at 1-2 items while I still have context on what you're working on."

7.4 Common Pushback and Responses

"I might need it later"

Access can be re-requested when a specific need comes up -- typically within [X hours]. Keeping access "just in case" for months is unnecessary risk.

"Everyone on my old team had this"

Role transfers are the #1 source of access buildup. What was right for your old role is not necessarily right for your current one.

"It's too much hassle to request every time"

If someone needs recurring access, the IAM team can set up a proper role for them. That is better than a permanent broad permission.

8. What Good Reviews Look Like

A quick note on review quality -- what the compliance team looks for and how to ensure your reviews count.

The goal of access reviews is not to revoke as much as possible. The goal is to make informed, documented decisions -- some approvals, some revocations, each with a reason.

What a Good Review Looks Like

IndicatorWhat It Shows
Each item reviewed individuallyYou considered each decision on its merits
Brief comments on non-obvious decisionsYour reasoning is documented for compliance
Mix of approvals and revocations over timeYou are genuinely evaluating, not auto-approving
Completed before the deadlineTimely, not last-minute

What Raises Questions

Compliance teams monitor review patterns. The following patterns may trigger a follow-up conversation (not a punishment -- just a check-in):

PatternWhy It Raises Questions
Everything approved in under a minuteSuggests items were not individually evaluated
100% approval rate across many campaignsStatistically unlikely that no access ever needs adjustment
No comments on any decisionsNo documented reasoning for compliance records
The bottom line
Take a moment to look at each item, especially anything flagged as privileged. Add a brief comment when the reason for your decision is not obvious. That is all it takes to demonstrate a thoughtful review. Your decisions are part of the organization's compliance record, and a well-documented review protects both you and the company.

Why This Matters for Everyone

For Your Team

Clean access profiles mean less personal risk. If a team member's account is compromised, less access = less damage and less time spent in forensic interviews.

For You

A documented, recent review decision with a clear comment is your best defense if anyone ever asks "why does this person have this access?"

For the Organization

Daily reviews provide continuous compliance evidence. Regulatory requirements (SOX, SOC 2, ISO 27001) require documented access review -- this is how we meet them.

10. Glossary

Plain-English definitions for terms you will encounter in the review process.

Access Profile
A bundle of permissions from one system. For example, "Standard CRM Access" might include 3 specific CRM permissions grouped together.
Approve
Your confirmation that a person should keep their access. Nothing changes -- the access stays in place.
Birthright Access
Access automatically given based on someone's job title or department. You cannot change it through a review -- it is controlled by HR/identity rules.
Campaign
A review cycle. You receive a campaign when your team members' access needs to be reviewed. Each campaign has a deadline.
Certification
Your specific review assignment within a campaign -- the list of people and access items you need to evaluate.
Daily Delta Review
A review that only covers access that changed in the last 24 hours (new permissions, not existing ones). "Delta" means "change."
Entitlement
A single, specific permission in a system. For example, membership in an Active Directory group called "CRM_Users" is one entitlement.
IAM Team
The Identity and Access Management team -- the IT group responsible for managing who has access to what. Contact them at [IAM-TEAM-EMAIL].
Identity
A person (your team member) as represented in the access management system. Sometimes you will see this term instead of "person" or "user."
Least Privilege
The principle that people should have only the access they need for their current job -- no more. Like only having keys to the rooms you actually use.
Privileged Access
Elevated permissions that let someone administer servers, manage user accounts, or access sensitive data organization-wide. Higher risk = more scrutiny.
Privilege Creep
The gradual buildup of access over time -- especially when people change roles but keep their old permissions. This is what daily reviews help prevent.
Reassign
Sending a review item to a different person who is better positioned to evaluate it. Not a failure -- it is the responsible choice when you lack context.
Revoke
Your decision to remove someone's access. After you sign off, the system begins removing it (automatically for connected systems, manually for others).
Role
A business-level access bundle that can span multiple systems. For example, "Customer Service Rep" might include CRM access + phone system access + knowledge base access.
Sign-Off
Clicking "Finish" to submit your final decisions. After this, your review is complete and cannot be changed. Your decisions are permanently recorded.
SOX / SOC 2
Regulatory standards (Sarbanes-Oxley and Service Organization Control) that require organizations to document access reviews. Your reviews are part of this evidence.
Source
A connected system -- like Active Directory, your CRM, or your HR system -- from which user accounts and permissions are imported into SailPoint.
